Writing education is difficult almost everywhere.

Teachers feel exhausted.
Students feel confused.
Feedback cycles feel endless.

This is usually explained as a problem of skill,
motivation, or talent.

This chapter argues something different:

Writing education is painful because completion is undefined.

The hidden problem in most writing courses

In many writing classes, students are asked to:

submit drafts

revise repeatedly

respond to feedback

improve over time

What they are rarely told is:

When is the work considered complete?

Without a clear answer,
both students and teachers operate in ambiguity.

Teachers are forced into subjective judgment

When completion is undefined,
teachers must decide:

whether a piece is “good enough”

whether enough effort was shown

whether revision should continue

These decisions are inherently subjective.

As a result:

grading becomes inconsistent

feedback becomes defensive

evaluation becomes exhausting

The problem is not the teacher.
It is the structure.

Students cannot aim at an invisible target

Students struggle for the same reason.

If completion criteria are unclear,
students do not know:

when to stop

how to prioritize feedback

what counts as success

They learn to negotiate judgment
instead of completing work.

This is not a failure of learning.
It is a failure of definition.

Feedback replaces completion

In many classrooms, feedback becomes a substitute for completion.

Work moves through cycles of:

draft → comment → revision → comment → revision

With no final state.

This creates the illusion of progress
without the experience of completion.

Over time, both sides burn out.

Why quality-first grading makes things worse

Grading writing by quality alone
forces evaluation too early.

Before completion is confirmed,
quality judgments are unstable.

Students respond by:

chasing preferences

second-guessing expectations

avoiding risk

Learning slows down
because completion never solidifies.

Completion must precede evaluation

This book proposes a reversal:

First, confirm completion.
Then, discuss quality.

When completion is system-defined:

teachers stop arguing about “enough”

students stop guessing expectations

feedback becomes constructive

Completion becomes a shared fact,
not a negotiated outcome.

What Route A changes in education

Route A introduces a minimal, objective requirement:

Run the system.
Generate verifiable output.

Nothing else is evaluated at this stage.

This single rule:

reduces grading conflict

restores student agency

lowers teaching fatigue

Most importantly,
it gives students their first real experience of finishing.

Teaching becomes possible again

Once completion is externalized:

assignments become scorable

progress becomes visible

responsibility becomes clear

Writing education does not become easy—
but it becomes workable.

And that is a profound change.

The principle to remember

If writing education feels painful,
do not first ask who is failing.

Ask instead:

Has completion been clearly defined?

Without that definition,
no amount of feedback will help.
